## Part 1 — The Deep Dive: what actually helps 4–7 year-olds learn

### The honest headline about "IQ"
There is strong evidence that good games improve **specific skills** in this age range — executive function, early math, literacy, and spatial reasoning. There is **weak evidence** that any game reliably raises a child's general IQ score. Brain-training studies repeatedly show gains on the trained skill (and sometimes near-transfer to related skills), but rarely a lift in global intelligence. So the right design target isn't "raise IQ" — it's "build the specific foundational skills that predict school success and that genuinely transfer." Those skills are below.

### The 4 skill areas with the best evidence

**1. Executive Function (EF) — the #1 trainable lever at this age.**
EF has three parts: **inhibitory control** (resisting an impulse / not doing the obvious thing), **working memory** (holding and updating info in your head), and **cognitive flexibility** (switching rules or perspectives). Early childhood is a peak window for EF growth, and multiple controlled studies show short, playful EF training improves working memory and self-control, with gains persisting ~3 months and transferring to untrained tasks. Well-developed EF in preschool predicts later academic *and* life success. This is the single most defensible thing a game can target.

**2. Early numeracy — the strongest single predictor of later achievement.**
A child's math knowledge at school entry predicts later math *and* reading better than almost anything else. The high-value early-math skills are **number sense** (knowing quantities and how they relate), **subitizing** (instantly seeing "how many" without counting), counting/cardinality, **comparison** (more/less, bigger/smaller), and **patterning** (the seed of algebra). Note: pure arithmetic drills matter less than this underlying "feel for number."

**3. Early literacy — a small set of high-leverage predictors.**
The best predictors of later reading are **alphabet/letter knowledge**, **phonological awareness** (hearing and manipulating sounds — rhymes, syllables, first sounds), **phonics/decoding** (blending letter-sounds into words), **rapid automatic naming**, **print knowledge**, and **vocabulary**. The recommended sequence is letter names → letter sounds → blending simple words → a few high-frequency sight words. Decoding (sound-blending) unlocks hundreds of words; sight words are a small add-on for irregular spellings.

**4. Spatial reasoning — the quiet STEM super-predictor.**
Spatial skills at age 3 predict arithmetic at age 10 and strongly predict who pursues STEM later. Crucially, spatial skills are **highly trainable** — a meta-analysis found a large average effect size (~0.96) for spatial training. The proven activities are **block building, puzzles, shape composition, mental rotation, and map/route reading.** This is underused and high-payoff.

*(Social-emotional learning and curiosity/science matter too, and your catalog already covers them well — see gaps below.)*

### What makes an early-childhood game actually work (design rules)
These come straight from the educational-game-design literature and AAP guidance, and should be requirements for every new game:

- **Immediate, clear feedback.** Every action gets an instant, obvious response (sound, animation, character reaction). This feedback loop *is* the learning.
- **Low floor, high ceiling.** A 4-year-old succeeds on turn one; a 7-year-old still gets challenged. Adaptive difficulty beats fixed levels.
- **Scaffolding that fades.** Heavy hints early (highlight the answer, slow it down), then gradually remove support as the child improves.
- **No-punishment exploration.** Mistakes are gentle and reversible — try-again, not game-over. This builds persistence.
- **Short loops, big payoffs.** Sessions of 1–3 minutes per round; celebration moments (confetti, collectibles) for intrinsic motivation. AAP suggests ~1 hr/day of quality content for ages 2–5, and context matters more than minutes — so design for short, satisfying, "good-stopping-point" rounds.
- **Multisensory + embodied.** Tap, drag, rotate, listen, repeat-a-beat. Hands-on and audio-rich beats passive watching, especially for pre-readers (audio instructions are essential — many players can't read yet).
- **One skill per game, clearly.** Each game should have a single, nameable learning target so it's easy to scaffold and easy for parents to understand.

2. **Subitizing / number sense** (you have addition, but nothing for instant quantity)
3. **Inhibitory control** — Go/No-Go, "Simon Says" self-control (Stop the Fire is speed, not impulse control)
4. **Cognitive flexibility** — rule-switching / sort-by-changing-rules
5. **Patterning** — AB/ABB/growing patterns (the algebra seed)
6. **Computational thinking** — sequencing/coding a character through steps
7. **Spatial navigation** — mazes, route planning, map reading
8. **Fractions / equal sharing** — part-whole intuition
9. **Rhythm & auditory sequence** — beat/sound memory (boosts phonological awareness + timing + working memory)
10. **Narrative sequencing** — putting story events in order (comprehension + temporal logic)

## Part 2 — 10 Game Ideas

*Each is designed to (a) fill a real gap, (b) target one evidence-backed skill, (c) have a fun, mechanically-sound core loop, and (d) fit your existing offline, no-ads, audio-friendly, emoji-bright style.*

---

### 1. 🤖 Robo Recess
**Skill:** Computational thinking + spatial planning (sequencing, debugging, mental rotation)
**Gap filled:** Coding / sequencing (none currently)

**The idea:** A friendly robot is stuck on a grid and wants to reach its star (or banana, or puppy). The child builds a little program from picture-blocks — ⬆️ forward, ↩️ turn left, ↪️ turn right — then presses the big green **GO** button and watches the robot follow the steps exactly.

**Core loop:** Drag step-blocks into a "command strip" → press GO → robot moves one block per step → succeed (confetti + collect star) or bump a wall and waddle back so the child can fix the sequence ("debugging" without the scary word).

**Why it's fun:** The delight is in pressing GO and watching your plan come alive — and the comedy when the robot cheerfully walks into a wall. Kids replay constantly to get the "perfect path."

**Scaffolding / progression:** Level 1 = 2 steps in a straight line, no turns. Then introduce a single turn, then obstacles, then "collect 3 stars in one program," then a **repeat** block (loop) for the 6–7 crowd. Optional star-count goal ("can you do it in 4 steps?") adds a high ceiling.

**Build notes:** Pure grid logic, no physics. Reuses your card-drag patterns (Shape Shifter / Animal Kingdom Sort). Audio: robot beeps per step so pre-readers can follow.

### 2. 🔤 Sound Soup (Blend-a-Beast)
**Skill:** Phonics decoding / sound-blending (the skill that unlocks reading)
**Gap filled:** Decoding (you have spelling & hangman, not blending)

**The idea:** Three letter-tiles drop into a bubbling pot — e.g. **c · a · t**. Tap each tile to hear its *sound* ("kuh" … "aa" … "tuh"), then tap the pot to **blend** them — the sounds speed up and smoosh together ("c-a-t… cat!") and a goofy creature (a CAT) pops out of the soup.

**Core loop:** Hear/segment the three sounds → blend → reveal the matching picture/creature → collect it in your "monster cookbook." Pure CVC (consonant-vowel-consonant) words.

**Why it's fun:** The blend animation is the payoff — sounds physically crash together and *poof*, a creature. Collecting the cookbook of silly beasts drives replay.

**Scaffolding / progression:** Start with picture shown + sounds auto-played (recognition). Then child taps to hear sounds themselves. Then "build the word for this picture" (choose tiles). Word families grouped (-at, -an, -ig) so patterns click. Later: 4-sound words and simple digraphs (sh, ch).

**Build notes:** Needs clean phoneme audio per letter (record once). Strong fit with Spell-a-Bee's audio engine.

### 3. ✨ Blink Count
**Skill:** Subitizing + number sense (instant quantity recognition)
**Gap filled:** Number sense (you have addition, not "how many at a glance")

**The idea:** A cluster of fireflies (or stars, or frogs) **flashes on screen for about a second**, then dims. The child taps the numeral that matches how many they saw — *before* counting one-by-one is possible. Catching the answer fast earns a glow bonus.

**Core loop:** Brief flash of N objects → choose from 2–3 numerals → instant feedback (fireflies re-light and "count themselves" to confirm). Quantities 1–5 early, up to 10 later.

**Why it's fun:** The "I just *knew* it was 4!" feeling is genuinely satisfying, and the short flash makes it feel like a magic trick / superpower. Time pressure is light and celebratory, never stressful.

**Scaffolding / progression:** Start with dice/domino arrangements (familiar patterns subitize easily), then scattered/random arrangements (harder), then "which group has *more*?" (comparison), then quick "5 and 2 more" to seed addition. Flash duration shortens as skill grows.

**Build notes:** Trivial to build — timed reveal + tap. Big learning payoff for low effort. Pairs naturally as a "warm-up" before Car Math.

---

### 4. 🦖 Dino Don't!
**Skill:** Inhibitory control (Go/No-Go self-regulation — core executive function)
**Gap filled:** Impulse control (Stop the Fire is speed, not inhibition)

**The idea:** Friendly dinos pop up one at a time. **Feed the green dinos** (tap them — they're hungry!) but **DON'T tap the red dinos** (they're full and grumpy). The whole game is about resisting the urge to tap everything.

**Core loop:** Dino appears → decide tap / don't-tap in ~1.5s → green tapped = yum + point; red *not* tapped = "phew!" + point; red tapped = gentle "oops, he was full!" and a giggle, no penalty beyond missing the point.

**Why it's fun:** The challenge is purely the impulse to tap — kids *know* the rule and still get fooled, which is hilarious and addictive. The "almost tapped it!" tension is the whole game.

**Scaffolding / progression:** Start with mostly green (easy go-trials), then increase red frequency, then speed up, then add a rule-flip twist for older kids ("now feed the RED ones"). This is a textbook Go/No-Go EF task dressed as a dino picnic.

**Build notes:** Very simple — timed spawns + tap detection. One of the most evidence-pure EF designs available.

### 5. 🎩 Switcheroo Sort
**Skill:** Cognitive flexibility / task-switching (rule-shifting)
**Gap filled:** Flexibility (no rule-switching game currently)

**The idea:** Cards with colored shapes (a red star, a blue star, a red circle…) slide in, and a magic hat tells you the **sorting rule**: "Sort by **COLOR**!" Drag cards to the matching bins. Then — *poof* — the hat changes the rule: "Now sort by **SHAPE**!" The child must drop the old rule and follow the new one.

**Core loop:** See current rule (big icon + audio) → sort 4–6 cards → rule flips → keep sorting. Score for sticking to the *current* rule.

**Why it's fun:** The "wait, the rule changed!" moment is a playful brain-twister. The hat character makes the switch feel like a magic trick rather than a test.

**Scaffolding / progression:** This is the classic **Dimensional Change Card Sort** (a gold-standard flexibility measure) turned into a game. Start with long stretches before a switch, lots of warning. Then switch more often, add a third dimension (size), then mixed/surprise switches for 6–7s.

**Build notes:** Reuses Animal Kingdom Sort's drag-to-bin mechanic. Mostly content + rule-state logic.

---

### 6. 🚂 Pattern Express
**Skill:** Patterning / early algebra (recognizing and extending patterns)
**Gap filled:** Patterning (Four Circles is logic, not sequence-patterns)

**The idea:** A train is being built and the cars follow a pattern — 🔴🔵🔴🔵❓ — what color is the next car? The child taps the right car to keep the train going, and it chugs off happily when complete.

**Core loop:** See a partial pattern → choose the next element from 2–3 options → train extends with a satisfying choo-choo → next pattern.

**Why it's fun:** Completing a pattern and watching the train roll is deeply satisfying to this age group, and patterns scale endlessly. Kids feel clever spotting the rule.

**Scaffolding / progression:** AB (red-blue-red-blue) → AABB → ABC → ABB → **growing** patterns (1 block, 2 blocks, 3 blocks…) → "fill the *middle* gap" not just the end (harder). Later: make-your-own pattern mode (creativity + self-test).

**Build notes:** Simple tap-to-place. Could share art with a "build mode." Strong, underrated math-readiness skill.

---

### 7. 🗺️ Mailbot Maze
**Skill:** Spatial navigation + route planning + map reading
**Gap filled:** Mazes / maps (World Explorer is facts, not navigation)

**The idea:** A little mail truck has to deliver a letter to a house across town. The child traces a path (finger-drag) through a maze of streets, avoiding puddles and dead ends, sometimes picking up a package on the way.

**Core loop:** See start + goal → drag a route → truck drives it → success or "oops, dead end, try again." Optionally plan the route first, *then* press go (planning > trial-and-error).

**Why it's fun:** Mazes are inherently satisfying, and the delivery theme gives purpose ("the puppy is waiting for his mail!"). Picking up packages adds a mini-collection goal.

**Scaffolding / progression:** Big simple mazes → tighter mazes → "collect all 3 packages then deliver" (route optimization) → a tiny top-down **map** the child reads to choose turns (map literacy, a proven spatial booster). 6–7s can get "shortest path" star challenges.

**Build notes:** Grid or path-based; finger-drag with snapping. Moderate art (a town tileset). High spatial payoff.

---

### 8. 🍕 Fair Share Pizzeria
**Skill:** Fractions / equal sharing / part-whole intuition (division readiness)
**Gap filled:** Fractions (none currently)

**The idea:** Hungry friends sit at the table and the child must **share food equally**. "3 friends, 1 pizza — give everyone a fair slice!" Cut the pizza (tap slice-lines) and drag equal portions to each plate. Everyone smiles when it's fair; someone frowns if they got less.

**Core loop:** See N friends + the food → cut/divide → distribute → fairness check (faces react). Introduces halves, thirds, quarters concretely.

**Why it's fun:** Fairness is *emotionally* gripping for this age — kids care intensely about equal shares. The friends' happy/sad reactions are immediate, meaningful feedback.

**Scaffolding / progression:** Start "share 4 cookies between 2 friends" (whole-object splitting, easy). Then 1 pizza / 2 friends = halves. Then quarters, thirds. Then "is this fair?" yes/no judging rounds (recognizing equal vs unequal). Later: connect to the words "half," "quarter."

**Build notes:** Drag-and-distribute + simple slice geometry. Ties thematically to Coin Counter Cafe (run-a-shop vibe).

---

### 9. 🥁 Echo Beats
**Skill:** Auditory sequence memory + rhythm (working memory + phonological/timing skills)
**Gap filled:** Rhythm / auditory memory (Memory games are visual)

**The idea:** A band of animals plays a short rhythm/sound pattern — 🐸*ribbit* 🐤*tweet* 🐸*ribbit* — and the child **taps it back** in order. Get it right and the pattern grows by one. A musical "Simon" with animal sounds.

**Core loop:** Watch/hear the pattern → repeat it by tapping the animals → correct = pattern grows + a richer beat builds; miss = the song "hiccups," try the same pattern again.

**Why it's fun:** It literally builds a song as you go — by round five the child has composed a little jam. Audio-driven, so it works great for non-readers and feels like making music, not a memory test.

**Scaffolding / progression:** 2-beat patterns → grow by one each success → add more instruments (more choices = harder) → introduce rhythm/timing (tap *in time*, not just order) for older kids. Auditory working-memory training also supports phonological awareness.

**Build notes:** Needs a small sound bank (use Tone.js or pre-recorded). Mechanically simple; high charm. Great accessibility for pre-readers.

---

### 10. 🎬 Story Scramble
**Skill:** Narrative sequencing + comprehension + temporal logic (first/next/last)
**Gap filled:** Sequencing/comprehension (Story Spark is choose-your-path, not ordering)

**The idea:** Three to five picture-cards from a tiny story are shuffled out of order — a seed 🌱, a sprout 🌿, a flower 🌸 — and the child drags them into the right order: what happened **first, next, last**. Then the cards animate into a little movie of the story.

**Core loop:** See scrambled panels → drag into order → tap "Play!" → if correct, the panels animate into a mini-movie with narration; if not, the wrong card gently wiggles back.

**Why it's fun:** The reward is watching your ordered story *become a movie* with sound. Kids love being the director, and the "cause-and-effect" stories (plant a seed → it grows) feel like little discoveries.

**Scaffolding / progression:** 3 cards with obvious order → 4–5 cards → stories where order is less obvious (logic, not just memory) → "tell me what happens" prompt encouraging the child to narrate (huge for oral-language development). Themes: growing, baking, getting dressed, weather.

**Build notes:** Drag-to-order + a simple panel-to-animation reveal. Reuses Story Spark's art/narration assets.
